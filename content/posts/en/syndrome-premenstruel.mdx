Tiredness, sore breasts, tears over a yoghurt ad, zero patience… then your period starts and everything settles. If that sounds familiar, you may know **premenstrual syndrome (PMS)**.

## What is PMS?

PMS is a set of **physical and emotional symptoms** that appear before a period and usually fade shortly after it starts. According to Inserm, the French national health research institute, PMS may affect **20 to 40% of people of reproductive age**, with very variable intensity. For about a third of them, it disrupts social, work or family life, and in about 5% of cases it is truly disabling.

It is not “in your head”, and it is not a matter of willpower.

## Common symptoms

- **Physical**: fatigue, bloating, tender breasts, headaches, back or lower-belly pain, digestive issues, skin problems.
- **Emotional**: feeling on edge, tension, irritability, anxiety, low mood, trouble concentrating.

## When does it happen?

PMS appears in the **luteal phase**, between ovulation and the period. Inserm notes that symptoms start from a few hours to several days before the period and usually disappear soon after it begins. Keeping a simple day-by-day record helps you spot that rhythm across cycles, and is useful to show a professional. A calendar never tells you how you feel today, though: only you can.

## What can help

There is no one-size-fits-all answer, and nothing here is medical advice. Inserm mentions lifestyle measures that help some people: regular sleep, regular physical and relaxing activities, less sugar, caffeine and alcohol. Lightening your schedule on harder days and telling your partner what would actually help (quiet, a hug, help with dinner) can also make a difference.

## When to see a professional

Talk to a doctor, gynaecologist or midwife if symptoms stop you from working or seeing people, if your mood becomes very dark, or if your relationships suffer every month. Only a health professional can make a diagnosis. When emotional symptoms are severe, it may be **premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)**.
